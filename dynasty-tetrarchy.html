Dynastic formation

Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius, Constantius and their rivals.

Political structure

Tetrarchy rulers relied on combinations of biological family, adoption, marriage alliances, military backing, senatorial recognition and court institutions.

Rulers

284–305

Diocletian

Created the Tetrarchy and radically reorganized imperial government.

286–305

Maximian

Diocletian's colleague in the Tetrarchic system.

305–306

Constantius Chlorus

Augustus of the West after the first Tetrarchic succession.

305–311

Galerius

Senior Tetrarch who fought Persia and administered the eastern empire.

306–307

Severus II

Tetrarchic Augustus defeated during Maxentius' revolt.

306–312

Maxentius

Ruler of Italy and Africa defeated by Constantine at the Milvian Bridge.

305–313

Maximinus Daia

Eastern Tetrarch and opponent of Constantine and Licinius.

285–286

Amandus

Bagaudic claimant in Gaul.

285–286

Aelianus

Bagaudic claimant associated with Amandus.

296–297

Domitius Domitianus

Egyptian rebel against Diocletian.

297–298

Achilleus

Egyptian rebel who continued resistance after Domitius Domitianus.

308–310

Lucius Domitius Alexander

African governor who revolted against Maxentius.

306–312

Maxentius' rival Constantine?

Cross-reference only; main profile is Constantine I.

Succession

Succession crises reveal the limits of dynastic labels. Co-emperorship, adoption, military proclamation and civil war repeatedly changed the line of succession.

Material culture

Coins and monuments helped present dynastic continuity through portraits of emperors, heirs, wives and children.
